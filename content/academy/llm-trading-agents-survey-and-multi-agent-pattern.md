# LLM Trading Agents: A 27-Paper Survey and the Multi-Agent Trading-Firm Pattern

[FinRobot's layered platform](/academy/financial-ai-agents-auditable-decisions) and the [general agent architecture](/academy/ai-agent-architecture-model-tools-orchestration) already covered in this series describe how an LLM agent reaches a decision. Two more papers widen the lens: a survey of 27 papers on LLM agents specifically applied to financial trading, and TradingAgents, a framework that organizes an agent's decision-making around a different structural idea entirely — the roles and internal debate of a real trading firm, rather than a single reasoning agent with tools.

## What a 27-paper survey of LLM trading agents finds

Ding, Li, Wang, Chen, Guo and Zhang pose the field's actual motivating question directly: can LLM agents outperform professional traders? They treat it explicitly as an open question, not one their survey answers definitively. Reviewing 27 papers that study LLMs for financial trading — seven of which use the term "agent" in their own title — the survey summarizes common architectural patterns, the data inputs used (news, prices, fundamentals, social sentiment), and the backtested performance reported across the literature, while also naming the field's real, unresolved challenges rather than only compiling positive results.

That last point matters for how to read the survey. A literature review of 27 self-reported backtests is, by construction, a summary of what each individual paper claims about itself — the survey can compare architectures and report what each paper states, but it cannot independently audit whether any individual paper's backtest would survive the kind of degradation check covered in [this series' overfitting research](/academy/stock-prediction-research-and-the-overfitting-trap). The survey's value is in mapping the design space and naming open problems, not in certifying that any specific reviewed system is production-ready.

## TradingAgents: an architecture built around disagreement

TradingAgents proposes a structurally different pattern from a single agent calling tools. It assigns LLM-powered agents specialized roles mirroring a real trading firm — fundamental analysts, sentiment analysts, technical analysts — then adds **Bull and Bear researcher agents** whose explicit job is to argue opposing views on market conditions, a dedicated risk-management team that monitors exposure, and traders with varied risk profiles who synthesize the debate and historical data into a final decision. This is a meaningfully different mechanism for surfacing disagreement and uncertainty than a single reasoning chain: instead of one model talking itself into a conclusion, the architecture structurally forces an opposing case to be made before a trade decision is reached, closer to how a real investment committee operates than to how a solitary analyst works. The paper reports improvements in cumulative returns, Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown relative to baseline models in its own backtests, and the framework's code is publicly available.

## Why the architectural choice itself is not a safety mechanism

More agents debating does not, by itself, guarantee more calibrated risk-taking. A "risk management team" role inside an LLM multi-agent framework is still, mechanically, more LLM output — text generated by a model playing a role — not a deterministic risk control, unless that output is validated and enforced by code outside the model, exactly as the [general agent architecture](/academy/ai-agent-architecture-model-tools-orchestration) article covers for the Functions pattern. A bear-case agent that is systematically less persuasive than the bull-case agent (because of prompt design, model bias, or simply which argument happened to generate more convincing-sounding text) can produce confidently wrong consensus just as easily as a single agent can, and the added structure also adds real cost: more model calls mean more latency, more inference cost and more surface area where one role's output can silently bias another's, none of which is addressed by having more roles rather than better-enforced boundaries.

## The practical boundary that still applies

The same capability-level framing already established for financial AI agents — Observe, Analyze, Recommend, Simulate, Execute, with authority defaulting to the lowest level required — applies without modification to a multi-agent trading-firm pattern. A TradingAgents-style "risk team" can productively sit at the Analyze or Recommend level, surfacing structured findings and flagging concerns for review. It should not be mistaken for [WolfBot's own deterministic risk controls](/docs/risk-controls), which exist specifically because LLM output, however well-structured the role prompting around it, is not a substitute for code-enforced position limits, TP/SL and exposure guards.

## What the papers show — and do not

The survey shows that LLM trading agents are an active, structurally varied research area with real, unresolved challenges, reviewed across 27 papers — it does not independently certify any individual reviewed system's backtest, and the central question it poses (can LLM agents beat professional traders) remains explicitly open. TradingAgents shows that a multi-agent architecture modeled on a real trading firm's role structure, with built-in bull/bear debate, can outperform baseline models in its own backtests — it does not show that the architectural pattern itself, independent of code-enforced risk controls outside the agent, is sufficient for managing real capital safely.
